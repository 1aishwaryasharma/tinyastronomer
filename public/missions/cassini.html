The spacecraft

A robot as big as a school bus

Cassini stood nearly 7 metres tall and weighed 5.7 tonnes at launch. NASA built the orbiter, the European Space Agency built the Huygens probe on its side, and Italy built the big dish.

Pick a part to look closer. Drag the spacecraft to turn it.

Two spacecraft in one

The orbiter carried twelve science instruments and the probe carried six more. Scientists and engineers from 27 countries worked on the mission.

15 October 1997 – 1 July 2004

The long way round

No rocket could throw something this heavy straight to Saturn. So Cassini went the other way first. It swung past Venus twice, then Earth, then Jupiter, and each planet's gravity flung it onward.

1 July 2004 · Saturn

Braking to stay

Cassini arrived far too fast to stay. It shot up through a gap in the rings, pointed its engine forward and fired for 96 minutes. That slowed it just enough for Saturn's gravity to catch it.

At its closest it skimmed 20,000 km above the cloud tops. It would not come that close again for almost thirteen years.

✦ FirstCassini became the first spacecraft to orbit Saturn. Three earlier ones had only flown past.
25 December 2004

Letting Huygens go

After seven years bolted to Cassini's side, the Huygens probe was pushed gently away by springs. It had no engine and no way to steer.

It was already aimed at Titan, still more than 2 million km away. It coasted there for three weeks, switched off except for three alarm clocks.

14 January 2005 · Titan

Landing on a hidden world

Huygens hit Titan's thick, smoggy air at 6 km every second. A heat shield and then three parachutes slowed it. It floated down for about two and a half hours, photographing hills and dry river channels.

It landed with a thump on damp ground scattered with pebbles of ice, and its data kept arriving for 72 minutes, until Cassini, its radio link to Earth, sank below the horizon.

✦ RecordIt is still the most distant landing ever made, and the only one in the outer solar system.

2004 – 2017

Thirteen years of orbits

Cassini never flew the same loop twice. Each pass by Titan, the orange dot, bent its path. Mission planners used the big moon like a steering wheel: tipping the orbit up over Saturn's poles, flattening it again, and swinging it out to other moons.

The mission was planned to last four years. It was extended twice, long enough to watch Saturn's seasons change from northern winter to northern summer. Counts are NASA's, from the end of the mission.

28 October 2015 · Enceladus

Flying through a fountain

Enceladus is a small, bright moon, 500 km across. In 2005 Cassini found jets of water vapour and ice spraying from cracks at its south pole. Under the icy shell is a salty ocean that wraps the whole moon.

On this pass Cassini flew straight through the spray, 49 km above the ground, and tasted it.

✦ DiscoveryThe spray held hydrogen gas: a sign of hot springs on the sea floor, and a kind of food that microbes on Earth can live on.

April – September 2017

The Grand Finale

Almost out of fuel, Cassini used one last Titan flyby to hop over the rings. Then it dived 22 times through the gap between the rings and the planet, a space about 2,000 km wide where no spacecraft had ever been, at 34 km every second.

On 15 September 2017 it did not come out the other side. Cassini flew into Saturn, still sending data, and burned up like a meteor.

Last signal sent10:32 UTC
Heard on Earth83 min later

The crash was on purpose. A dead spacecraft might one day hit Enceladus or Titan and carry Earth germs to a place that could have life of its own.

What it left us

Two moons worth going back to

Cassini took 453,048 pictures and changed the list of places where life might be found. Titan has rain, rivers and seas, made of liquid methane. Enceladus has a warm ocean.

NASA is building Dragonfly, a car-sized, nuclear-powered drone that will fly from place to place on Titan. As of November 2026 its launch is planned for July 2028, with arrival in 2034.

— Guide for kids, parents & teachers —

Cassini for kids: the spacecraft that lived at Saturn

Cassini–Huygens was a robot spacecraft built by NASA, the European Space Agency and the Italian Space Agency. It launched in 1997, reached Saturn in 2004 and circled the planet for thirteen years, studying its rings and moons, until it was steered into Saturn in 2017.

Why did it take seven years to reach Saturn?

Cassini was one of the heaviest spacecraft ever sent to the outer planets, and no rocket was strong enough to send it there directly. Instead it borrowed speed from planets. Each time it swung close behind a moving planet, that planet's gravity pulled it forward and it left faster than it arrived. Two flybys of Venus, one of Earth and one of Jupiter added up to the speed it needed. The trip covered 3.5 billion kilometres.

What did Huygens find on Titan?

Titan is Saturn's largest moon, bigger than the planet Mercury, and the only moon with a thick atmosphere. Orange haze hides its surface. Huygens parachuted through that haze in January 2005 and photographed a landscape that looked oddly like Earth: hills, a shoreline, and channels carved by flowing liquid. The liquid is not water. Titan is about −180 °C, cold enough for methane, which is a gas on Earth, to fall as rain and fill lakes and seas.

Why is Enceladus so exciting?

Life as we know it needs liquid water, the right chemical ingredients and a source of energy. Cassini found signs of all three at Enceladus. Jets at the south pole spray water from an ocean under the ice into space, where a spacecraft can sample it without landing. Cassini found salts, carbon-rich molecules and hydrogen in the spray. Nobody has found life there. Enceladus is simply one of the best places to look.

What are the parts of the spacecraft?

  • High-gain antenna. A 4-metre dish for talking to Earth, also used as a radar and sometimes as a dust shield.
  • Huygens probe. A 2.7-metre lander with a heat shield, parachutes and six instruments.
  • Nuclear batteries. Three generators that turned the heat of plutonium-238 into electricity, because sunlight at Saturn is too weak for solar panels of that era.
  • Magnetometer boom. An 11-metre arm holding magnetic sensors away from the spacecraft.
  • Cameras and telescopes. Fixed to the body, so the whole spacecraft turned to point them.
  • Radio and plasma antennas. Three 10-metre rods for listening to radio waves and counting dust hits.
  • Main engines. Two, with one as a spare, used for the big braking burn at Saturn.

Why was Cassini crashed on purpose?

By 2017 its fuel tanks were nearly empty. Without fuel, engineers could no longer steer it, and one day it might have crashed into Enceladus or Titan. Cassini was not perfectly clean; a few hardy microbes from Earth may still have been aboard. To keep those ocean worlds untouched for future explorers, the team sent Cassini into Saturn, where it burned up in under two minutes. On the way in it measured the planet's atmosphere directly for the first time.

Learning goals

  • Trace Cassini's route and explain why it flew toward the Sun before heading out to Saturn.
  • Read a speed-against-time chart and link each jump to a gravity assist.
  • Name the three things life needs and say which of them Cassini found at Enceladus.

Try this in class

  1. Scroll through “The long way round” and stop at each flyby. Ask students to read the speed before and after from the chart, and to explain why the speed drops in between.
  2. Signal maths: the page shows how long a radio signal takes from Saturn right now. If you sent a command and Cassini answered at once, when would the reply arrive?
  3. In “Thirteen years of orbits”, pause in each colour and describe how the shape and tilt of the orbits changed. What might the team have wanted to see from above the poles?
  4. Hold a class vote before reading the last chapter: crash Cassini into Saturn, or leave it in orbit? Vote again afterwards.

Discussion questions

  • Huygens had no engine. How could it reach Titan?
  • Titan has rain and rivers but is far too cold for liquid water. What does that tell us about what makes a world “Earth-like”?
  • Is it right to destroy a working spacecraft to protect a place that may have no life at all?
