The telescope

A telescope on a sunshade

Webb is a gold mirror 6.5 metres wide standing on a silver sunshield as long as a tennis court. At launch it had a mass of about 6,200 kilograms.

Built to stay cold

Webb looks for faint heat from far away, so nearly every part has one job: keep the mirror and its cameras dark, steady and very, very cold.

25 December 2021 – 24 January 2022

A million miles uphill

Webb does not circle Earth. The rocket threw it toward a point 1.5 million km away, four times as far as the Moon. Like a ball rolled up a hill, it slowed all the way: from 6.6 km/s an hour after launch to 0.2 km/s a month later.

NASA's broadcast of the launch, from French Guiana.

The throw was a little too gentle on purpose. Webb can push itself faster, but it can never turn around to brake: that would point its cold mirror at the Sun.

24 January 2022 – today

Orbiting nothing

There is nothing at L2. It is a balance point, where the pull of the Sun and Earth together carries a spacecraft around the Sun in step with Earth. From there the Sun, Earth and Moon are always on the same side, behind the sunshield.

Webb loops around that empty point about twice a year. The balance is like a ball on a hilltop, so about every three weeks it fires small thrusters to keep from rolling off.

The sunshield

Hot side, cold side

Warm things glow in infrared, and that includes telescopes. To see faint heat from space, Webb has to be far colder than the coldest place on Earth.

Its sunshield is five sheets, each about as thin as a hair. Each one is cooler than the one below, and heat escapes sideways through the gaps.

The golden eye

Eighteen mirrors acting as one

Each hexagon is 1.32 metres across, made of light, stiff beryllium and coated in gold a thousand times thinner than a hair. Together they catch about six times as much light as Hubble's 2.4-metre mirror.

Seeing heat

Light your eyes cannot see

Infrared is light with longer waves than red. Dust clouds block ordinary light, but infrared slips through, so Webb can watch stars being born inside them.

Visible light: the dust cloud is a dark wall, and the stars behind it are hidden.

Infrared also shows the distant past. Light from the first galaxies set out more than 13 billion years ago, and the stretching of space has stretched it from visible light into infrared.

IllustrationAccuracy & sources

The picture behind this card is drawn by this page, not taken by Webb. It shows one true idea: dust dims short waves of light far more than long ones, so the same cloud that hides stars in visible light is nearly clear in infrared. The cloud, the stars and the colours are made up, and infrared has no colour of its own.

12 July 2022 – today

What it has found so far

Webb's first pictures were shown to the world on 12 July 2022. Since then it has:

  • found carbon dioxide in the air of a planet around another star (2022);
  • measured a galaxy as it was only about 290 million years after the Big Bang (2024);
  • shown newborn stars and planet-forming discs hidden inside dust clouds.

It was built to work for at least five years. After launch, NASA worked out that its fuel should last much longer than ten.

James Webb Space Telescope for kids: how it works and where it is

The James Webb Space Telescope, often just called Webb or JWST, is a space telescope built by NASA with the European and Canadian space agencies. It launched on 25 December 2021 and studies the universe in infrared light from about 1.5 million kilometres away.

Where is the Webb telescope?

Webb is not in orbit around Earth like the Hubble Space Telescope. It circles the Sun alongside Earth, near a place called the second Lagrange point, or L2. L2 lies about 1.5 million kilometres from Earth on the side facing away from the Sun, roughly four times as far as the Moon. Webb does not sit still there. It follows a wide loop around L2 about once every six months, and its distance from Earth swings between about 1.2 and 1.75 million kilometres.

Why did Webb have to unfold?

Its mirror is 6.5 metres across and its sunshield is 21 metres long, but the nose cone of its Ariane 5 rocket was only about 5 metres wide. So the telescope was designed to fold. Over the two weeks after launch it opened in steps: the sunshield pallets, a tower that lifts the telescope, the five sunshield layers, the secondary mirror, and finally two wings of the main mirror. A total of 178 release devices had to let go, and all of them did.

What are the parts of the telescope?

  • Primary mirror. Eighteen hexagonal segments of beryllium, each 1.32 metres across and coated with a layer of gold 100 nanometres thick. Gold reflects infrared light very well.
  • Secondary mirror. A round mirror 74 centimetres wide on three long legs. It sends the gathered light back through the middle of the main mirror.
  • Sunshield. Five thin layers of a plastic called Kapton, 21 by 14 metres. It keeps the heat of the Sun, Earth and Moon off the telescope.
  • Science instruments. Four of them, behind the mirror: cameras, and spectrographs that spread light into its colours to show what things are made of.
  • Spacecraft bus. The warm side below the sunshield, with the computers, radio, fuel and thrusters.
  • Solar panel and antenna. The panel makes electricity. The dish sends at least 28.6 gigabytes of data to Earth twice a day.

How cold is Webb?

The Sun-facing side reaches about 85 °C. The mirror side stays near −233 °C, only 40 degrees above the coldest temperature possible. One instrument, which sees the longest waves, is chilled even further by a special refrigerator, to about −266 °C.

Why infrared?

Infrared light passes through dust that blocks visible light, so Webb can see into the clouds where stars and planets form. Cool objects, such as planets, glow mostly in infrared. And light from the earliest galaxies has been stretched by the expansion of space until it arrives as infrared, which is why a telescope built to see the young universe must see heat.

Learning goals

  • Explain why Webb had to fold, and name the main steps of its unfolding.
  • Describe where L2 is and why Webb's sunshield works there.
  • Explain why infrared light shows things visible light cannot.

Try this in class

  1. Scroll through the unfolding together. Pause before each step and ask what students think has to move next, and what would go wrong if it stuck.
  2. Mirror maths: Webb's mirror is 6.5 m across and Hubble's is 2.4 m. How many times wider is Webb? (About 2.7.) Why does it collect about six times the light? (Area grows with the square of the width, less the gaps.)
  3. Origami challenge: fold a sheet of paper so it fits in a matchbox and opens flat in one pull. Compare designs.
  4. Use the infrared slider, then ask what a firefighter's heat camera and Webb have in common.

Discussion questions

  • Hubble was repaired by astronauts. Why can nobody repair Webb?
  • Why must the telescope itself be cold to see heat?
  • When Webb shows a galaxy 13 billion light-years away, are we seeing it as it is now?
