The spacecraft

A robot the size of a small car

Voyager 1 has a mass of about 800 kilograms. It has no solar panels, because sunlight is far too weak where it travels. Its electricity comes from the heat of slowly decaying plutonium.

Pick a part to look closer. Drag the spacecraft to turn it.

Built to last five years

The mission was planned to study Jupiter and Saturn and then end. Its builders gave every important system a spare. Nearly fifty years later, it is still reporting back.

5 September 1977

Aim for where Jupiter will be

Voyager 1 left Florida on a Titan-Centaur rocket, moving fast enough to escape Earth. It did not fly straight at Jupiter. It was thrown onto a long curve to meet the planet eighteen months later, where Jupiter was going to be.

Its twin, Voyager 2, had launched sixteen days earlier on a slower path. Voyager 1 overtook it on the way, which is how it earned the “1”.

5 March 1979 · Jupiter

Stealing speed from a giant

Voyager 1 swung around Jupiter 349,000 km from its centre. Jupiter races around the Sun at 13 km every second, and its gravity towed the little spacecraft along with it. Voyager arrived moving about 13 km/s and left at about 24. This trick is called a gravity assist.

Speed relative to the Sun, from JPL's trajectory. The spike is Voyager falling toward Jupiter and climbing back out.

✦ DiscoveryIts cameras caught volcanoes erupting on the moon Io: the first active volcanoes ever seen beyond Earth.
1979 – 1980

Twenty months to Saturn

Coasting uphill against the Sun's pull, Voyager slowed from 24 km/s to 20. Even so, it crossed the gap between Jupiter and Saturn, wider than the whole distance it had flown so far, in under two years.

12 November 1980 · Saturn

One close look, and no way back

Scientists had a choice. Voyager 1 could go on to more planets, or pass very close to Titan, the only moon with a thick atmosphere. They chose Titan.

The spacecraft skimmed past the orange moon, dipped under Saturn's south pole, and was flung upward, out of the flat plane where the planets orbit. It has not visited a world since.

✦ DiscoveryTitan's air is mostly nitrogen, like Earth's, and so hazy that the cameras could not see the ground.
14 February 1990 · 6 billion km out

The Pale Blue Dot

High above the planets, Voyager 1 turned its camera around one last time and took a family portrait of the solar system: sixty frames, six planets.

In one frame, in a beam of scattered sunlight, is a speck smaller than a single pixel. That speck is Earth. Every person who has ever lived, lived there.

Minutes later the cameras were switched off for good, to save power for the long trip ahead.

2004 – 2012

Leaving the Sun's bubble

The Sun blows a thin wind of charged particles in every direction, puffing up a huge bubble called the heliosphere. Outside it is the even thinner gas that drifts between the stars.

Wind suddenly slows16 Dec 2004 · 94 AU
Edge of the bubble25 Aug 2012 · 122 AU

One AU is the distance from Earth to the Sun. Crossing that edge, the heliopause, made Voyager 1 the first human-made object in interstellar space. Nobody knows the bubble's true shape; the one drawn here is a simple guess.

18 November 2026

One light-day from home

Light is the fastest thing there is: it reaches the Moon in just over a second. On 18 November 2026 Voyager 1 became the first spacecraft so far away that light, and every radio message, needs a whole day to get there.

A message in a bottle

The Golden Record

Bolted to Voyager's side is a gold-plated copper record, made in case anyone ever finds it. A team led by Carl Sagan filled it with the sights and sounds of Earth.

The cover is engraved with picture instructions for playing it, and a map that points to our Sun using fourteen flashing stars called pulsars.

What's next

Going quiet, going on

Voyager's nuclear batteries make a little less power every year. Engineers have switched off instruments one by one; as of November 2026 two are still listening. Some time in the 2030s the last one will fall silent.

The spacecraft will keep coasting. In about 300 years it reaches the Oort Cloud, a swarm of icy comets around the Sun. In about 40,000 years it drifts past another star, Gliese 445, still carrying its record.

— Guide for kids, parents & teachers —

Voyager 1 for kids: the farthest spacecraft from Earth

Voyager 1 is a robot spacecraft that NASA launched in 1977. It flew past Jupiter and Saturn, kept going, and is now farther from Earth than anything else people have built: more than 25 billion kilometres away.

How far away is Voyager 1?

So far that kilometres stop being useful. Astronomers measure it in light-travel time instead. Light crosses 299,792 kilometres every second, and it still needs a full day to reach Voyager 1. When engineers send a command, they wait a day for it to arrive and another day for the answer. The spacecraft adds about 1.46 million kilometres to its distance from the Sun every day.

How does a gravity assist work?

A planet is moving around the Sun. A spacecraft that passes behind a planet is pulled forward by the planet's gravity and leaves with some of the planet's motion added to its own. Seen from Jupiter, Voyager 1 arrived and left at the same speed. Seen from the Sun, it left nearly twice as fast. Jupiter slowed down in exchange, by an amount far too small to measure.

What are the parts of the spacecraft?

  • High-gain antenna. The white dish is 3.7 metres across and always points at Earth. Its transmitter is about as strong as a refrigerator light bulb.
  • Nuclear batteries. Three generators turn the heat of decaying plutonium-238 into electricity. They made about 470 watts at launch and lose a few watts each year.
  • Magnetometer boom. A 13-metre arm holds magnetic sensors far from the spacecraft's own electronics.
  • Scan platform. A turning mount for the two cameras and other light-measuring instruments. All of them are now switched off.
  • Plasma wave antennas. Two 10-metre rods that sense vibrations in the thin electrified gas around the spacecraft.
  • The bus. The ten-sided body holds the computers, a digital tape recorder and the fuel tank for its small thrusters.

Has Voyager 1 left the solar system?

Not yet, and it depends what you mean. In 2012 it left the heliosphere, the bubble blown by the solar wind, so it is now in the space between the stars. But the Sun's gravity still holds a vast, distant swarm of comets called the Oort Cloud. Voyager 1 will need about 300 years to reach its inner edge and perhaps 30,000 years to fly through it.

What is the Golden Record?

Each Voyager carries a 30-centimetre gold-plated copper disc with 115 pictures, greetings in 55 languages, natural sounds and 90 minutes of music from many cultures. Its cover shows how to play it. Space is so empty that the record should last for more than a billion years.

Learning goals

  • Describe Voyager 1's route and explain why it visited only two planets.
  • Explain a gravity assist as a transfer of motion from a moving planet.
  • Use light-travel time to compare very large distances.

Try this in class

  1. Scroll to the Jupiter flyby together. Before it plays, ask students to predict whether the spacecraft will end up faster or slower, then read the speed chart.
  2. Light-time maths: light takes about 8 minutes from the Sun to Earth and 24 hours to Voyager 1. How many times farther is Voyager? (About 173 times.)
  3. Press “Say hello to Voyager” and work out when a reply could come back. Compare it with a message to the Moon, which takes 1.3 seconds each way.
  4. Make a class Golden Record: each student chooses one picture, one sound and one greeting, and explains the choice.

Discussion questions

  • Why can't Voyager 1 use solar panels?
  • Was choosing Titan over more planets the right decision? What was gained and lost?
  • How would you explain Earth to someone who has never seen it?
